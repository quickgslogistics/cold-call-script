Cold Open (Warm-Up)

Before any selling — you need to get past the fight-or-flight reflex. Your opener has ONE job: make them lower their guard long enough to hear one question. Confused + low-energy wins every time.

TONE: Confused · Low energy · Almost like wrong number · No agenda
Step 1 — The Confused Opener
You
"Yeah… is this uh… is this [First Name]?"
Upward inflection — like you're not sure you have the right person. This alone stops the "not interested" reflex before it starts.
Prospect
"Yeah, who's this?"
You
"Oh hey… yeah it's just uh… [Your Name]… I was wondering if you could possibly um… help me out for a moment."
The word "just" is intentional — it downplays you. On a cold call you have zero credibility already. "Just" signals it's not a big deal → they lower their guard. "Help me out" triggers human instinct to assist.
Prospect
"Uh… sure, how can I help you?"
Step 2 — "Not sure if you're the right person" + Hidden Gap + Consequence
You
"Well… and I'm not sure if you're the right person I should be talking to… but I called to see who would be responsible for… overseeing any possible hidden gaps in like your dispatching — like finding loads and negotiating rates with brokers — that could be causing you guys to keep leaving money on the table every month… uh… who should I be talking to about that?"
Works for both owner-ops (1–2 trucks) and fleets (3+ trucks). For fleets adjust to: "…causing your fleet to leave money on the table every month…"
"Possible" = neutral word — they can't say "we don't have that" because you said possible. "Could be" = neutral, not "is." "Who should I be talking to" = if they ARE the owner, they can't say "that's not me" without looking bad. They say "that's me."
Their reaction — pick the scenario
Prospect
"That's me — what's this about?"
You
"Oh good… yeah so um… we work with [equipment] carriers like [Company] out of [state] on the dispatching side — finding loads, negotiating rates, handling all the paperwork… uh… I'm not even sure if what we do would make sense for [trucks] or if you've already got that covered… would you be opposed to me asking you just a couple questions to find out?"
"I'm not even sure if what we do would make sense" = push them away → they pull you back. "Would you be opposed" = uses their instinct to say no in your favor. → Move to Stage 1: Connection.
Prospect
"What do you mean by hidden gaps?"
You
"Yeah good question… so what I mean is like… most owner-operators I talk to are spending a lot of hours every week finding loads on DAT or Truckstop, negotiating with brokers, chasing invoices… and they don't always realize how much that's costing them in rates they're not getting and time they're not driving… uh… is any of that something you're dealing with?"
Curiosity triggered ✅ — they asked YOU a question. Now describe their problem in their language. End with a yes/no question to confirm fit. → Move to Stage 1.
Prospect
"Not interested."
You
"Oh yeah… no that's fair… uh… I haven't even really told you what it's about yet honestly… I'm just trying to figure out if it even applies to your situation… would you be opposed to me asking you just one quick question? And if it doesn't fit I'll let you go right there."
"I haven't even told you what it's about yet" — said lightly, not defensive. They fired before hearing anything. This resets the conversation to neutral. One question is all you need.
Prospect
"I'm kind of busy right now."
You
"Oh no, totally… uh… when would be a better time to reach you — is later today okay or is tomorrow better for you?"
Binary callback — never "I'll try you another time." Two options, both move forward. Most people pick one. Note the time immediately.
Prospect
"Oh — you want [Name]. I'm just the driver / dispatcher / office person."
You
"Oh got it — thanks for letting me know. Is there any chance you could put me through to them? Or if they're not available, I can leave a voicemail and they can reach me when it's a good time."
Always try for the transfer first. The person answering may actually have more influence than you think — be respectful and professional with them.
You (if they transfer)
"Hey [Owner Name]… yeah it's uh… [Your Name] from QuickGS… [first name] mentioned you'd be the right person… uh, I was just wondering if you could possibly help me out for a moment…"
Voicemail — if they're unavailable
You
"Hey [Owner Name]… this is [Your Name] from QuickGS. [first name] mentioned you're the one running the operation at [Company]. I had a specific question — not a sales pitch — about how [trucks] on [equipment] out of [state] are currently getting their loads covered. Takes 2 minutes. My number is (307) 509-5759 — again, (307) 509-5759. I'll try you back. No pressure."
Keep it under 20 seconds. Don't mention what you do or what you're selling — curiosity is the hook. The double number repetition is intentional — they're usually driving. Key elements: their name twice, colleague reference for credibility, specific but vague hook ("dispatching side"), number twice, promise to call back so they know this isn't a one-shot robocall.

When transferred, use the gatekeeper's first name immediately. It creates instant credibility — you're not a random cold caller, you're someone they were connected to.
🔒 A receptionist, assistant, or employee answers — not the owner. Goal: get transferred without triggering a hard no.
Gatekeeper
"[Company name], how can I help you?"
You
"Oh hey yeah… this is [Your Name]… uh… is [Owner Name] around by any chance?"
Name the owner directly. Don't explain why you're calling — that gives the gatekeeper a reason to screen you. Be casual, not formal.
Gatekeeper
"Can I ask what this is regarding?"
You
"Yeah it's just about their trucking operation — I had a quick question for them. It'll only take a second."
Vague but honest. "Their trucking operation" is specific enough to be credible but vague enough to not be screenable. "Quick question" signals low pressure.
Gatekeeper
"They're not available right now. Can I take a message?"
You
"Sure — just let them know [Your Name] from QuickGS called. My number is (307) 509-5759. I'll also try back later — when's usually a good time to reach them?"
Always ask for the best time. The gatekeeper usually knows the owner's schedule and will tell you. This doubles your callback success rate.
✅ They said "call me back Tuesday" — this is a warm call, not cold. Adjust tone: slightly more confident, reference the prior conversation.
You
"Hey [Name]… yeah it's [Your Name] from QuickGS… we spoke [last week / a few days ago] and you mentioned this was a better time to connect… uh… still okay to chat for a few minutes?"
Reference the previous conversation immediately — it establishes credibility and reminds them who you are. They agreed to this call, so the guard is already lower.
Prospect
"Oh yeah, right. Sure, go ahead."
You
"I appreciate that. So when we talked before you mentioned [specific thing they said — fleet size / pain point]. I just wanted to pick up from there — has anything changed since then, or is that still the situation?"
Referencing what they said previously shows you listened and took notes. This is the single biggest trust-builder on a callback call.
Prospect
"Hey I got a missed call from this number — did you call me?"
You
"Oh yeah — yeah I did try to reach you… uh… this is [Name] from QuickGS Logistics… and uh… I'm glad you called back actually. I was wondering if you could help me out for a moment — I'm not sure if you're the right person I should be speaking with, but I was looking into dispatching-related stuff for owner-operators in your area… uh… are you the one running the trucking side?"
Returned call = much warmer than a cold call. They called YOU back. Stay slightly uncertain but you can be more direct. Their calling back is an implicit signal of curiosity.

01

Connection

Make them feel safe. Get permission to ask questions. Transition from curiosity to trust. They must feel you are different from every other salesperson.

TONE: Warm · Slow · Slightly confused · No urgency
✅ Picking up right after the warm-up opener — prospect just said: "No I'm not opposed, what do you got?" or "Go ahead."
You
"Yeah… and honestly, before I get into anything — I want to make sure I'm even talking to the right person, and that what we do would actually make sense for your situation. Would you be okay if I just asked you a couple quick questions first?"
This signals you're not in pitch mode. Asking permission is disarming — almost nobody says no to this.
Their response — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Yeah sure, go ahead."
You
"I appreciate that. And just so you know — I'm not here to sell you anything today. If after we talk it doesn't make sense for you, I'll be the first one to say it. Sound fair?"
"I'm not here to sell you anything" — the brain releases oxytocin instead of cortisol. Guard drops completely. This sentence is worth memorizing.
Prospect
"Yeah, that's fair."
Prospect
"I'm kind of busy right now — make it quick."
You
"Totally — I won't take much of your time. Honestly I just need about 2 minutes to see if what we do even makes sense for your setup. And if it doesn't, I'll let you go right there. Is that fair?"
Don't rush your pace. Ironically, slowing down when they say "make it quick" communicates calm confidence and makes them relax.
Prospect
"Wait — who exactly is this? Where did you get my number?"
You
"That's totally fair to ask — I'm [Your Name] from QuickGS Logistics. We work with [equipment] carriers in [state] and across the lower 48. I was actually looking at your operation — you're registered as [Company], DOT [DOT], [trucks] — I'm not trying to waste your time at all — would you be okay if I just asked you one quick question to see if this even applies to you?"
Be direct and transparent. Hiding how you got the number creates distrust. Name the source calmly and pivot back to permission.
⚠️ They're telling you they're NOT the decision-maker. Your goal: transfer to the right person OR leave a voicemail and send an SMS so the right person can reach you.
Prospect
"Oh, that's not me — you want to talk to [Name / the owner / my boss]."
You
"Oh got it — thanks for letting me know. Hey, before I let you go — do you think you could transfer me over to them? And if they're not available, I'm totally fine leaving a voicemail so they can reach me back when it's a better time."
Always ask for the transfer first. If they can't transfer, voicemail + SMS is your fallback. Never just hang up — you lose the lead entirely.
Prospect
"Sure, let me see if they're available / Yeah I can transfer you / They're not in right now."
If transferred — restart with the owner
You (when transferred)
"Hey [Name]… yeah it's uh… [Your Name] from QuickGS Logistics… I was just talking to [their colleague] and they mentioned you'd be the right person to speak with… I'm not even sure if what we do applies to you at all, but I was hoping maybe you could just point me in the right direction?"
Fresh start with the owner. Mention the colleague's name for instant social proof. Then go right back into the warm-up script.
If they're NOT available — Voicemail script
You (Voicemail)
"Hey [Name]… this is [Your Name] from QuickGS Logistics. [colleague name] mentioned you run [Company] — [trucks] on [equipment] out of [state]. I think there's money being left on the table on the rate side and I had one specific idea I wanted to run by you — takes 2 minutes. Call me at (307) 509-5759 — again that's (307) 509-5759. I'll also try you tomorrow. Thanks [Name]."
Why this voicemail works (proven method):
1. Name twice — first and last line. People perk up when they hear their name.
2. "[colleague name] mentioned you" — warm referral signal. Not a cold robocall. They're already curious.
3. Specific fleet data — trucks + equipment + state. Impossible to fake. They know you actually looked them up.
4. "Money being left on the table" — every carrier believes this. It's the universal pain trigger.
5. "One specific idea" — not "a service" or "a solution". An idea. Low threat, high curiosity.
6. "Takes 2 minutes" — removes fear of commitment.
7. Number twice, slowly — they're driving. Pause between digits.
8. "I'll also try you tomorrow" — persistent but not desperate. Shows you mean it.
9. Name again at the end — closes the loop, feels personal not scripted.
Target: 15–20 seconds max. Anything longer gets deleted.

📱 Send this text the moment you hang up:
"Hey [Name], this is [Your Name] from QuickGS — just left you a voicemail. We work with [equipment] carriers in [state] and I had a quick idea for [Company] on the rate side. quickgslogistics.com — call or text anytime."
Prospect
"Can you call me back? Now's not a great time."
You
"Of course — I completely understand. Would later today or tomorrow morning work better for you?"
Binary choice. Never "whenever you're free." Lock in a specific time block so you're not chasing indefinitely.
Prospect
"Tomorrow morning works."
You
"Perfect — I'll reach out around [time]. And just so you have it in the meantime, I'll send you a quick text with our info — no pressure at all. Have a good one!"
Always send the SMS even before the callback. They may research you before you call back. This primes them and dramatically increases pickup rate.
Prospect
"Wait — is this a sales call? Because I'm not interested."
You
"Hah… I mean… kind of, yeah. But I haven't even told you what it's about yet… so I'm not sure if it applies to you or not. I was just hoping you could answer one quick question and if it doesn't fit I'll let you go right there. Would you be opposed to that?"
Honesty combined with humor disarms the reflex. You're not denying it — you're reframing it. "I haven't told you what it's about yet" resets them.

Transition line: "So let me ask you — just so I understand your situation better…" — signals a shift from rapport to discovery without breaking the flow.

Situation Questions

Map their current setup with neutral curiosity. Trucks, dispatching method, load boards, hours spent. No judgment. Every answer is ammo for the consequence stage.

TONE: Genuinely curious · Casual · Conversational
You
"So just to get a better picture — how many trucks are you running right now?"
You already know: [X trucks] — [utilization]. Skip this question — instead say: "I see you're running [X trucks] out of [state] — is that still the case?" This signals you did your homework.
Their fleet size — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Right now I've got three. Working on getting a fourth."
You
"Got it. And with [trucks] running [equipment] out of [state] — are you handling all the dispatching yourself, or do you have someone helping with that?"
This version already uses their data. If they seem surprised you know — that's good. Just say: "I was reviewing your FMCSA profile before calling." Shows professionalism and preparation. The more they explain, the more invested they become in the conversation.
Prospect
"Mostly myself. Sometimes my wife helps when it gets busy."
You
"Okay. And for finding [equipment] loads out of [state] — are you mainly on [load boards] yourself, working through brokers, or a mix of both?"
Prospect
"Load boards mostly. DAT, Truckstop. I call brokers too when the boards are slow."
You
"Makes sense. And roughly how many hours a week would you say you're spending on all of that — finding loads for [trucks], calling brokers, handling paperwork? Because for a [fleet-adj], that's usually around [time-cost]."
Critical setup question. Their answer will feed Stage 5 (Consequence). Pause after asking — let them do the math out loud.
Prospect
"We've got five trucks on the road right now."
You
"Nice, solid operation. And with five trucks — are you managing dispatch yourself or do you have a dedicated person on that?"
Prospect
"I have one person helping but honestly they're stretched thin. I still end up handling a lot of it."
You
"Yep that's super common when you're at that size. And in terms of the load mix — are you mainly on load boards, direct shippers, or a combination?"
At 4–6 trucks they're at the inflection point. The pain of managing growth is real. Lean into that in Stage 3.
Prospect
"Just me. One truck. I'm an owner-operator."
You
"Got it. So you're doing everything — driving, finding loads, paperwork, calling brokers — all on your own?"
Prospect
"Yeah pretty much. It's a lot."
You
"I bet. And when you're on the road — are you able to be actively looking for loads at the same time, or does one always take a backseat to the other?"
For solo operators the time/driving conflict is their core pain. That's your entire Stage 3 angle.
Prospect
"I've got two trucks and I handle everything myself. No dispatcher."
You
"Makes sense — a lot of guys at your stage are in the same spot. And when it comes to load boards — do you feel like you're getting good visibility on the right lanes, or does it feel like you're kind of fishing in the dark sometimes?"
Validate the no-dispatcher setup before probing. Never make them feel wrong for how they're operating.
Prospect
"I just got my authority about two months ago. Still figuring everything out."
You
"Nice — congrats on that. Starting out is a lot. And right now, how are you finding your loads — mainly load boards, or have you started building broker relationships yet?"
New carriers are extremely open to help — they don't have bad habits yet and they're actively looking for guidance. This is high-quality opportunity. Go slower, be extra consultative.
Prospect
"Mostly load boards. I've been trying to call brokers but it's tough to get them to take me seriously with no track record."
You
"Yeah that's the classic new carrier problem. And how's the rate negotiation side going — are you getting close to what you think the lanes should actually pay, or do you feel like you're kind of taking whatever they offer?"
Prospect
"We're running eight trucks right now, plus I've got two more coming off the lot next month."
You
"That's a solid operation. And with ten trucks inbound — how's your dispatching team set up? Do you have dedicated dispatchers for each truck or are they covering multiple?"
At this size the conversation shifts to operational efficiency and dispatcher quality. Their pain is management overhead and load consistency across multiple trucks — not just time. Pitch dispatcher as a scalable solution: "we can cover all your trucks under one dispatcher or assign dedicated dispatchers per truck depending on volume."
Prospect
"One dispatcher covering all of them. It's getting pretty hectic."
You
"Yeah, one dispatcher for eight trucks is a lot. And when they're stretched like that — where does the quality start slipping first? Is it the rates, the turnaround time on finding loads, or something else?"
Reference their mileage: "I see [Company] reported about [mileage] last year — that's a lot of [equipment] miles to keep fully dispatched with one person." — FMCSA data shows this, say it with confidence.
🌉 BRIDGE → Problem Awareness
Closing question to transition: "And with everything you just described — the dispatching, finding loads, handling brokers — how's that been working out for you overall?" — this opens the door to the pain.

Problem Awareness

You don't tell them they have a problem — they discover it through your questions. Use "how's that been working for you" style questions. Never lead with a negative assumption.

TONE: Empathetic · Slightly slower · Head-nodding energy
You
"And with all that time you're putting in on [trucks] — how's that been working for you overall? Like with [equipment] out of [state] — are you getting the rates you want, or does it feel like you're leaving some money on the table sometimes?"
"How's that been working for you" is the most powerful NEPQ opener. It sounds conversational but opens the floodgates.
Their pain level — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Honestly it's hit or miss. Some weeks are great, other weeks the rates are garbage and I'm just trying to keep the truck moving."
You
"Yeah, I hear that. When the rates are low like that — what do you usually end up doing? Do you take the load anyway just to keep rolling, or do you sit and wait?"
Prospect
"I usually take it. Can't afford to sit. But sometimes I feel like I'm just covering fuel."
You
"Got it. And when you're dealing with brokers — how often do you feel like you're getting the rate you actually wanted versus having to settle for whatever they offer?"
Prospect
"Maybe half the time. Brokers know how to lowball. And it takes forever to negotiate."
You
"And what about the paperwork side — BOLs, invoices, following up on payments — how's that running? Is it pretty smooth or does that eat up a chunk of your time too?"
Prospect
"That's probably the worst part. I've had brokers take 60, 90 days to pay. I hate chasing that stuff."
Prospect
"Honestly it's been okay. I can't complain too much."
You
"Good to hear. And when you say okay — like are you hitting the revenue targets you set for yourself, or is it more like 'okay for now, but I know there's more out there'?"
Never accept "okay" as the final answer. There's almost always a gap between "okay" and "what I actually want." This question finds it without creating friction.
Prospect
"The second one. I know there's room to grow but I just don't have the bandwidth right now."
You
"Yeah — that bandwidth thing. What's eating up most of your time right now that you feel like is keeping you from getting to that next level?"
Prospect
"Man, it's been rough. Rates are terrible, brokers are lowballing everybody, I'm barely breaking even some weeks."
You
"I hear that — and honestly that's more common right now than people talk about. Can I ask — is it more the rate side, or is it also time — like the hours you're spending just trying to find decent loads?"
When they're in pain, go slower. Lower your voice slightly. Let the weight of their situation sit before jumping in. Don't rush to solutions.
Prospect
"Both. I spend hours on DAT just to get lowballed anyway. It's exhausting."
You
"Yeah. And when you're putting in all those hours — how much of that time is actual driving versus admin, phone calls, and chasing brokers?"
Prospect
"The biggest thing killing me is the payments. Brokers take forever to pay. I've got money tied up for months."
You
"That's a real cash flow killer. And when you say forever — are we talking 30 days, 60 days, or even longer? What's the worst one you've dealt with?"
Get them to quantify the pain. The bigger the number, the easier the ROI conversation in Stage 6.
Prospect
"Had one go 90 days last year. I almost couldn't make payroll."
You
"Wow. And has that changed anything about how you pick which brokers you work with — or do you still kind of have to take whatever's available?"
Prospect
"My biggest problem is deadhead. I'm running too many empty miles positioning for loads."
You
"That's eating into your margins bad. And when you're repositioning — is that usually because you couldn't find a backhaul, or because the backhauls available were just not worth the rate?"
Deadhead pain leads directly into the value of a dispatcher who specializes in lane optimization. Stay in curiosity mode.
Prospect
"Both. The boards in certain regions are just dead and I end up driving home empty."
You
"Yeah that's rough. And if you had to put a rough number on it — what would you say you're losing per month in empty miles alone?"
🌉 BRIDGE → Solution Awareness
Closing question to transition: "So based on what you're describing — if you had someone handling all of that for [trucks], what would that actually change for you?"

Solution Awareness

Get them to describe the ideal solution in their own words. When they articulate what they want, they are selling themselves. You are just holding the mirror.

TONE: Reflective · Open · Let silence breathe after questions
You
"So let me ask you something — what would it look like for you if all of that was just… handled? The [equipment] load finding for [trucks], the broker calls, the paperwork, the invoicing — if someone was doing all of that for you — what changes for you day to day?"
This is the pivot question of the entire call. Pause after you say it. Give them space. Their answer will be more powerful than any pitch you could deliver.
Their vision — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Man… I'd just drive. That's what I want to do. I'm good at driving, not at being on the phone all day."
You
"That makes total sense. And if you had someone negotiating [equipment] rates on your behalf out of [state] — someone who knows what every lane is worth and pushes back on brokers — do you think that would make a meaningful difference on your revenue every week?"
Prospect
"Yeah for sure. I know I'm leaving money out there. I just don't have the time to fight for it."
You
"And what about your time — you're spending roughly [time-cost] on dispatching and admin for [trucks] — what would you actually do with that time?"
Opportunity cost question. Shifts their thinking from "savings" to "growth."
Prospect
"Probably get that fourth truck going sooner. That's been my goal for a year now."
Prospect
"Honestly I'd probably hire another driver and add a truck. That's the goal."
You
"That's a real goal. And what's the main thing standing between you and pulling that trigger right now — is it capital, or more that you're just too stretched managing what you have?"
Prospect
"Mostly the second one. I can barely manage three trucks efficiently right now. Adding another feels overwhelming."
You
"And if the operational side was handled — dispatch, rates, paperwork — do you think you'd feel ready to scale up?"
Prospect
"I mean… I'm not sure. Just less stress I guess?"
You
"Less stress — that actually says a lot. Where's most of the stress coming from right now? Like if you had to point to one thing, what is it?"
Vague answers mean they haven't fully felt the pain yet. Help them locate it specifically. Don't move to Stage 4 until you have a concrete problem they've named themselves.
Prospect
"Probably not knowing where my next load is coming from. The inconsistency kills me."
You
"Yeah that inconsistency is the real killer. And if you had consistent, well-priced loads lined up every week — what would that change for you practically?"
Prospect
"Honestly I'd just get some time back with my family. I feel like I'm always working."
You
"That's real. And when you say always working — is it the driving itself, or is it more the admin stuff that bleeds into your personal time?"
Personal pain is actually more powerful than business pain. Don't redirect to business logic — stay in this emotional territory. It converts higher.
Prospect
"The admin stuff mostly. I'm doing broker calls at 9pm after my kids go to bed."
You
"Yeah… and how long has that been the situation? Like is this a recent thing or has it been like that for a while?"
Prospect
"Honestly I just want to make more money. I feel like I'm working too hard for what I'm bringing in."
You
"That's fair. And when you say more money — is it more about getting better rates per load, running more miles, or more about not having to give so much of the margin away to brokers?"
Prospect
"All three honestly. But yeah, the broker margins are brutal."
You
"Yeah. And if you had someone pushing back hard on those brokers every load — knowing what every lane actually pays — do you think that would move the needle on your per-mile rate meaningfully?"

05

Consequence

Help them feel the cost of staying where they are. Not fear tactics — just honest future-pacing. The goal is for them to say "I don't want to stay here." They say it. You don't.

Tonality Guide

Jeremy Miner says tonality accounts for 50% of results. The words are 50% — how you say them is the other 50%. This guide covers every stage of the call.

REFERENCE — Not a stage, a cheat sheet
Tone by Stage
COLD OPENER (Warm-Up)
Confused · Relaxed · Slightly unsure
Low energy. Natural pauses. "Um…" and "uh…" are intentional. You are not sharp or energetic. You sound like you almost called the wrong number.
CONNECTION (Stage 1)
Warm · Genuine · Unhurried
Friendly but not salesy. Slow down 20%. Think of a trusted advisor, not a rep. "I appreciate that" — say it and mean it.
SITUATION (Stage 2)
Curious · Neutral · Like a journalist
You are genuinely learning, not fishing for angles. Head slightly tilted. "Got it… and…" — acknowledge, then move to the next question without pressure.
PROBLEM AWARENESS (Stage 3)
Empathetic · Slightly slower · Head-nodding
"I hear that a lot…" said slowly builds enormous rapport. When they share pain, pause for 2–3 seconds before responding. That silence communicates that you actually heard them.
SOLUTION AWARENESS (Stage 4)
Reflective · Open · Inviting
After "what would it look like for you if…" — go completely silent. Let them paint the picture. Do not fill the silence. This pause is where they sell themselves.
CONSEQUENCE (Stage 5)
Heavier · Slower · Let it land
Drop your voice slightly. Slow down noticeably. After "where do you see yourself in 12 months?" pause for 3–4 seconds. The weight of the question should settle before they answer.
COMMITMENT (Stage 6)
Calm · Steady · No urgency
You are not excited. You are settled. This communicates that you don't need the sale. Zero desperation. The energy of someone who has plenty of other clients and is genuinely checking fit.
PRESENTATION (Stage 7)
Confident · Conversational · Not a pitch
You're sharing information, not presenting. "We do X" not "Our patented solution delivers." Mirror their vocabulary back at them. Short sentences. Check in constantly.
OBJECTIONS (Stage 8)
Calm · Curious · Zero defensiveness
Objections are questions in disguise. Slow down MORE when they push back. Defensiveness = pitch mode. Curiosity = NEPQ mode. You want to understand, not counter.
CLOSE + SMS (Stage 9)
Light · Easy · No pressure
The close should feel like a natural next step, not a moment of tension. "I'll send you a text right now" removes all friction. The SMS does the heavy lifting after the call.
