01

Connection

Make them feel safe. Get permission to ask questions. Transition from curiosity to trust. They must feel you are different from every other salesperson.

TONE: Warm · Slow · Slightly confused · No urgency
✅ Picking up right after the warm-up opener — prospect just said: "No I'm not opposed, what do you got?" or "Go ahead."
You
"Yeah… and honestly, before I get into anything — I want to make sure I'm even talking to the right person, and that what we do would actually make sense for your situation. Would you be okay if I just asked you a couple quick questions first?"
This signals you're not in pitch mode. Asking permission is disarming — almost nobody says no to this.
Their response — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Yeah sure, go ahead."
You
"I appreciate that. And just so you know — I'm not here to sell you anything today. If after we talk it doesn't make sense for you, I'll be the first one to say it. Sound fair?"
"I'm not here to sell you anything" — the brain releases oxytocin instead of cortisol. Guard drops completely. This sentence is worth memorizing.
Prospect
"Yeah, that's fair."
Prospect
"I'm kind of busy right now — make it quick."
You
"Totally — I won't take much of your time. Honestly I just need about 2 minutes to see if what we do even makes sense for your setup. And if it doesn't, I'll let you go right there. Is that fair?"
Don't rush your pace. Ironically, slowing down when they say "make it quick" communicates calm confidence and makes them relax.
Prospect
"Wait — who exactly is this? Where did you get my number?"
You
"That's totally fair to ask — I'm [Name] from QuickGS Logistics, we work with owner-operators in your area. I got your info from [FMCSA / referral / load board]. I'm not trying to waste your time at all — would you be okay if I just asked you one quick question to see if this even applies to you?"
Be direct and transparent. Hiding how you got the number creates distrust. Name the source calmly and pivot back to permission.
02

Situation Questions

Map their current setup with neutral curiosity. Trucks, dispatching method, load boards, hours spent. No judgment. Every answer is ammo for the consequence stage.

TONE: Genuinely curious · Casual · Conversational
You
"So just to get a better picture — how many trucks are you running right now?"
Their fleet size — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Right now I've got three. Working on getting a fourth."
You
"Got it. And how are you currently handling your dispatching — are you doing it all yourself, or do you have someone helping with that?"
Don't assume. Let them describe it. The more they explain, the more invested they become in the conversation.
Prospect
"Mostly myself. Sometimes my wife helps when it gets busy."
You
"Okay. And for finding loads — are you mainly using the load boards yourself, working through brokers, or a mix of both?"
Prospect
"Load boards mostly. DAT, Truckstop. I call brokers too when the boards are slow."
You
"Makes sense. And roughly how many hours a week would you say you're spending on all of that — finding loads, calling brokers, handling paperwork?"
Critical setup question. Their answer will feed Stage 5 (Consequence). Pause after asking — let them do the math out loud.
Prospect
"We've got five trucks on the road right now."
You
"Nice, solid operation. And with five trucks — are you managing dispatch yourself or do you have a dedicated person on that?"
Prospect
"I have one person helping but honestly they're stretched thin. I still end up handling a lot of it."
You
"Yep that's super common when you're at that size. And in terms of the load mix — are you mainly on load boards, direct shippers, or a combination?"
At 4–6 trucks they're at the inflection point. The pain of managing growth is real. Lean into that in Stage 3.
Prospect
"Just me. One truck. I'm an owner-operator."
You
"Got it. So you're doing everything — driving, finding loads, paperwork, calling brokers — all on your own?"
Prospect
"Yeah pretty much. It's a lot."
You
"I bet. And when you're on the road — are you able to be actively looking for loads at the same time, or does one always take a backseat to the other?"
For solo operators the time/driving conflict is their core pain. That's your entire Stage 3 angle.
Prospect
"I've got two trucks and I handle everything myself. No dispatcher."
You
"Makes sense — a lot of guys at your stage are in the same spot. And when it comes to load boards — do you feel like you're getting good visibility on the right lanes, or does it feel like you're kind of fishing in the dark sometimes?"
Validate the no-dispatcher setup before probing. Never make them feel wrong for how they're operating.
03

Problem Awareness

You don't tell them they have a problem — they discover it through your questions. Use "how's that been working for you" style questions. Never lead with a negative assumption.

TONE: Empathetic · Slightly slower · Head-nodding energy
You
"And with all that time you're putting in — how's that been working for you overall? Like are you getting the rates you want, or does it feel like you're leaving some money on the table sometimes?"
"How's that been working for you" is the most powerful NEPQ opener. It sounds conversational but opens the floodgates.
Their pain level — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Honestly it's hit or miss. Some weeks are great, other weeks the rates are garbage and I'm just trying to keep the truck moving."
You
"Yeah, I hear that. When the rates are low like that — what do you usually end up doing? Do you take the load anyway just to keep rolling, or do you sit and wait?"
Prospect
"I usually take it. Can't afford to sit. But sometimes I feel like I'm just covering fuel."
You
"Got it. And when you're dealing with brokers — how often do you feel like you're getting the rate you actually wanted versus having to settle for whatever they offer?"
Prospect
"Maybe half the time. Brokers know how to lowball. And it takes forever to negotiate."
You
"And what about the paperwork side — BOLs, invoices, following up on payments — how's that running? Is it pretty smooth or does that eat up a chunk of your time too?"
Prospect
"That's probably the worst part. I've had brokers take 60, 90 days to pay. I hate chasing that stuff."
Prospect
"Honestly it's been okay. I can't complain too much."
You
"Good to hear. And when you say okay — like are you hitting the revenue targets you set for yourself, or is it more like 'okay for now, but I know there's more out there'?"
Never accept "okay" as the final answer. There's almost always a gap between "okay" and "what I actually want." This question finds it without creating friction.
Prospect
"The second one. I know there's room to grow but I just don't have the bandwidth right now."
You
"Yeah — that bandwidth thing. What's eating up most of your time right now that you feel like is keeping you from getting to that next level?"
Prospect
"Man, it's been rough. Rates are terrible, brokers are lowballing everybody, I'm barely breaking even some weeks."
You
"I hear that — and honestly that's more common right now than people talk about. Can I ask — is it more the rate side, or is it also time — like the hours you're spending just trying to find decent loads?"
When they're in pain, go slower. Lower your voice slightly. Let the weight of their situation sit before jumping in. Don't rush to solutions.
Prospect
"Both. I spend hours on DAT just to get lowballed anyway. It's exhausting."
You
"Yeah. And when you're putting in all those hours — how much of that time is actual driving versus admin, phone calls, and chasing brokers?"
04

Solution Awareness

Get them to describe the ideal solution in their own words. When they articulate what they want, they are selling themselves. You are just holding the mirror.

TONE: Reflective · Open · Let silence breathe after questions
You
"So let me ask you something — what would it look like for you if all of that was just… handled? The load finding, the broker calls, the paperwork, the invoicing — if someone was doing all of that for you — what changes for you day to day?"
This is the pivot question of the entire call. Pause after you say it. Give them space. Their answer will be more powerful than any pitch you could deliver.
Their vision — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Man… I'd just drive. That's what I want to do. I'm good at driving, not at being on the phone all day."
You
"That makes total sense. And if you had someone actually negotiating rates on your behalf — someone who knows what every lane is worth and pushes back on brokers — do you think that would make a meaningful difference on your revenue every week?"
Prospect
"Yeah for sure. I know I'm leaving money out there. I just don't have the time to fight for it."
You
"And what about your time — if you got back, say, 15 to 20 hours a week that you're currently spending on dispatching and admin — what would you do with that time?"
Opportunity cost question. Shifts their thinking from "savings" to "growth."
Prospect
"Probably get that fourth truck going sooner. That's been my goal for a year now."
Prospect
"Honestly I'd probably hire another driver and add a truck. That's the goal."
You
"That's a real goal. And what's the main thing standing between you and pulling that trigger right now — is it capital, or more that you're just too stretched managing what you have?"
Prospect
"Mostly the second one. I can barely manage three trucks efficiently right now. Adding another feels overwhelming."
You
"And if the operational side was handled — dispatch, rates, paperwork — do you think you'd feel ready to scale up?"
Prospect
"I mean… I'm not sure. Just less stress I guess?"
You
"Less stress — that actually says a lot. Where's most of the stress coming from right now? Like if you had to point to one thing, what is it?"
Vague answers mean they haven't fully felt the pain yet. Help them locate it specifically. Don't move to Stage 4 until you have a concrete problem they've named themselves.
Prospect
"Probably not knowing where my next load is coming from. The inconsistency kills me."
You
"Yeah that inconsistency is the real killer. And if you had consistent, well-priced loads lined up every week — what would that change for you practically?"
05

Consequence

Help them feel the cost of staying where they are. Not fear tactics — just honest future-pacing. The goal is for them to say "I don't want to stay here." They say it. You don't.

TONE: Slower · Measured · Heavy pauses · Let it land
You
"So you've been wanting that fourth truck for about a year now… what do you think has been getting in the way of making that happen?"
Don't fill this in for them. Pause. Let them arrive at the answer on their own. The silence is intentional.
Prospect
"Time and money, honestly. I'm so busy keeping these three running I never have a clear enough picture to make the move."
You
"Yeah that makes sense. And just thinking about the last few months — if you had to put a rough number on it — how much do you think you've lost in underpriced loads, deadhead miles, and slow-paying brokers? Just a ballpark."
Don't give them a number. Make them calculate it. Their number will always be more credible and more emotionally real than yours.
Prospect
"Ugh… honestly it could be several thousand a month. I don't even want to think about it."
You
"I get it. And if nothing changes — where do you see yourself with the business in the next 12 months? Still at three trucks, or…?"
Future-pacing. The "or…" trailing off gives them space to complete it.
Prospect
"Honestly, probably same spot. That's what I'm trying to avoid."
You
"Right. And is that something you're okay with… or is changing that actually important to you?"
The commitment seed. Forces them to take a verbal position.
06

Commitment

Verbal buy-in before the pitch. Summarize what they told you and let them confirm it. They earn their way to the presentation. If they're not committed here, don't pitch.

TONE: Calm · Confident · Measured · No rush
You
"So based on everything you just shared — the time you're spending, the rates you're missing, the slow payments, the fourth truck being on hold for a year — would you say solving this is actually a priority for you right now, or more of a 'someday when I get around to it' thing?"
"Priority vs someday" — makes them self-categorize. If they say someday, gracefully disqualify and move on. Don't chase maybes.
Their commitment level — pick the scenario
Prospect
"No, it's a priority. I'm tired of spinning my wheels."
You
"I appreciate you being straight with me. Let me show you exactly what we do — and you just tell me honestly if you think it fits your situation. Sound good?"
Only now do you earn the pitch. You've built full trust. The guard is down. This is the green light.
Prospect
"I mean… it's something I've been thinking about but I'm not sure the timing is right."
You
"That's fair. And just so I understand — what would have to change for the timing to feel right? Is it a money thing, a bandwidth thing, or something else?"
Never push a "maybe." Instead, get them to define what "right timing" looks like. If they can't define it, it's usually fear of change. Name that gently.
You (if they still stall)
"No worries at all. Would it be worth getting on a 15-minute call in a couple weeks when you've had a chance to think it over? No pressure — just so I can answer any questions you have."
Graceful re-schedule. Never burn a bridge on a "maybe." Follow up in 2–3 weeks.
Prospect
"It sounds good but honestly I'm not sure I can swing it right now financially."
You
"That's a real concern. Can I ask — is it more that you're not sure you'd make back what you spend, or is it genuinely a cash flow issue right now?"
Separate value doubt from cash flow. They're different problems. Value doubt can be addressed now. Cash flow might need a different structure (trial load, deferred payment).
You (ROI reframe)
"You mentioned losing several thousand a month in underpriced loads. If what we charge ends up being less than that — and most carriers see it pay for itself in the first 2 weeks — would that change how you're thinking about it?"
07

Presentation — "Present by not presenting"

Frame your services as the answer to what THEY described in their words. No feature dumping. Tie every point back to something they said. Check in every 2–3 sentences.

TONE: Confident · Steady · Conversational · Not a monologue
You
"So what we do at QuickGS is pretty simple. You said you want to just drive — that's exactly the idea. We take everything off your plate: finding loads, negotiating rates with brokers, handling BOLs, invoicing, and following up on payments. You focus on the road, we handle the business side."
Use their exact words back at them. "You said you want to just drive" — that's a mirror technique. They feel deeply understood.
You
"You mentioned getting lowballed on rates — our dispatchers negotiate full-time, that's all we do. We know what every lane is worth and we don't settle. Most of our carriers see their per-mile rate go up in the first two to three weeks."
You
"And on the payment side — we stay on brokers so you're not chasing invoices at 60 or 90 days. We keep your cash flow moving so you can think about that fourth truck instead of wondering when you're getting paid."
You — Check-in (never monologue more than 3 points)
"Does that sound like it actually addresses what you were describing — or is there something specific you'd want to know more about?"
Check-in question every 2–3 points. If you monologue, you become every other salesperson. Stay in dialogue mode the entire time.
Their reaction — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Yeah, that actually sounds pretty solid. How does it work exactly?"
You
"Simple setup — we assign you a dedicated dispatcher. They learn your lanes, your preferences, and handle everything from load search to broker negotiation to paperwork. You just drive and approve loads. We handle the rest."
Prospect
"What kind of lanes do you cover? I'm mainly running the Midwest."
You
"We cover all 48 states, but your dispatcher would focus specifically on your preferred lanes. So if you're running Midwest corridors — Chicago, Detroit, St. Louis, Kansas City — we know exactly what those lanes should pay and we push for it. Is Midwest mainly where your trucks run, or do you go further out sometimes?"
Answer the question directly, then bounce back with a question. Keeps the dialogue going and shows you actually know the market.
Prospect
"I've heard this pitch before. Everyone says the same stuff."
You
"That's fair — and I get it. I'm not going to tell you we're different, because everyone says that too. What I will say is — the best way to find out is to let us run one load for you. You see the rate we get, how we handle the broker, the paperwork. If it's not better than what you'd do yourself, we shake hands and go our separate ways. Would you be opposed to trying that?"
Don't argue with skepticism. Agree with it, then remove the risk entirely with a trial. "Would you be opposed" closes the loop.
08

Objection Handling — 3-Layer NEPQ Loop

Never argue. Never counter-pitch. Use 3 layers: (1) Validate + clarify, (2) Question back, (3) Reframe. Each layer is a question. They talk themselves out of their own objection.

TONE: Calm · Curious · Never defensive · Pause before Layer 2
Which objection are they throwing? — click to jump
Objection 1 — "I already have a dispatcher / I'm good right now"
I already have a dispatcher — I'm good right now.
L1
"Oh totally, I wasn't assuming you didn't. And I'm genuinely not here to replace something that's working well. Can I ask — just out of curiosity — how long have you been working with them?"
↳ Validate + gather info. Never challenge their current situation.
L2
"And in terms of rates — do you feel like they're consistently getting you what the lane is actually worth, or is there still some room there?"
↳ Specific gap question. Plant the seed without attacking.
L3
"What would have to be different about what you have now for you to feel like it was working at the level you actually want?"
↳ Let them define the gap. If they describe it, they own it.
Objection 2 — "What's your percentage / How much do you charge?"
What's your percentage? How much do you charge?
L1
"That's a fair question and I'll absolutely get into that. Before I do — I just want to make sure what we do even makes sense for your setup first."
↳ Defer price without dodging. Keeps context before numbers.
L2
"If the numbers ended up working out — and most of our carriers find it pays for itself in the first couple weeks through better rates — would pricing still be the main thing holding you back?"
↳ Conditional close. Isolate whether price is actually the issue.
L3
"We charge [X%] per load. Given everything you said about what you're currently losing in underpriced loads and slow payments — does that feel like it would actually be a net positive for you?"
↳ Now reveal price — framed against their stated losses. ROI becomes obvious.
Objection 3 — "Let me think about it / Send me some info"
Let me think about it… can you send me some info?
L1
"Of course, and I want you to feel comfortable. I'll send that over."
↳ Agree first. Never resist "think about it" — it creates pressure.
L2
"Can I ask though — when you say think about it, is there something specific that's making you hesitate, or is it more just not wanting to make a quick decision?"
↳ Uncover the real objection. "Think about it" is almost never about thinking.
L3
"I totally get that. What if we did this — let me set you up with one trial load, no commitment. You see exactly how we work, what rate we get you, and how we handle the paperwork. If it's not better than what you're doing now, we part ways with no hard feelings. Would you be opposed to that?"
↳ The trial offer removes all risk. "Opposed" framing closes the loop. Almost nobody says they're opposed.
Objection 4 — "I've had bad experiences with dispatchers before"
I've had bad experiences with dispatchers before.
L1
"I hear that — and honestly that's more common than it should be in this industry. I'm sorry that happened."
↳ Full validation. Don't be defensive. Don't say "we're different" yet — you haven't earned that.
L2
"What happened with them, if you don't mind me asking? I want to understand what went wrong — because if we have the same problem, I'd rather know now than waste your time."
↳ Makes them articulate the specific failure. Now you know exactly what to address.
L3
"So what would have to be true for you to feel confident enough to give it another shot — what does that look like for you?"
↳ They define what "trustworthy dispatcher" means. Now just confirm you match their criteria.
Objection 5 — "I can't afford it right now"
I don't know if I can afford it right now.
L1
"That's a real concern and I respect that. Can I ask — is it more of a cash flow issue right now, or is it more that you're not sure the value would be there?"
↳ Separate cash flow from value doubt. They're different problems requiring different responses.
L2
"You mentioned losing several thousand a month in underpriced loads and slow payments. If what we charge you ended up being less than that — would cost still be the issue?"
↳ ROI reframe using their own number from the consequence stage. This is why Stage 5 matters so much.
L3
"What if we started with just one truck on a trial basis, zero upfront — you only pay once we get you a load? That way there's no risk and you can see the value before committing anything."
↳ Reduce the perceived risk to near-zero. Start small, prove value, expand naturally.
Objection 6 — "I need to talk to my partner / spouse first"
I need to talk to my partner / wife first.
L1
"Absolutely, that makes total sense — especially if they're involved in the business. I'd expect nothing less."
↳ Validate completely. Never make them feel judged for involving a partner.
L2
"From your own perspective though — based on what we've talked about — does this feel like something that makes sense for where you want to take the business?"
↳ Separate their personal conviction from the approval process. If they say yes, now they're an internal champion.
L3
"Would it help if I jumped on a quick call with both of you — even 15 minutes — so I can answer any questions they might have directly? That way you're not playing phone tag trying to explain it."
↳ Offer the 3-way call. Gets you in the room with the real decision-maker without being pushy.
09

Commitment Close

Never push — pull. Let them close themselves. Your job is to make the next step feel easy, obvious, and low-risk. Binary choices only. No open-ended "when are you free?"

TONE: Calm · Confident · Unhurried · Zero desperation
You — Emotional Check
"So based on everything we've talked about — does this feel like something that could actually move the needle for you?"
"Feel like" = emotional language. Not "do you want to sign up." Let them respond emotionally first before you ask for a commitment.
Where are they? — pick the scenario
Prospect
"Yeah, honestly it sounds pretty good."
You — Define Their Next Step
"I appreciate that. And just so I'm clear — what would have to happen for you to feel comfortable moving forward? What does that look like on your end?"
Let them define the close. Whatever they say becomes the roadmap. You're not pushing — you're asking what they need to feel safe.
You — Binary Choice Close
"Perfect. What I'll do is send you a quick one-pager on exactly how it works, and we can get on a 15-minute call this week to get you set up. What works better for you — Wednesday or Thursday?"
Binary choice close. Never "when are you free?" — that's an open door to delay. Two options, both move forward. Most people choose one.
Prospect
"I'm interested but I want to see how it works first before committing."
You — Trial Close
"Here's what I'd suggest — let's get you set up with a no-risk trial load. We find you a load, negotiate the rate, handle all the paperwork, and you see exactly what it looks like in practice. If it goes well, we talk about working together on a regular basis. If not, no hard feelings — you owe us nothing. Would you be opposed to trying that?"
"Would you be opposed" — closes the loop using their instinct to say no. The trial eliminates every risk objection in one sentence.
Prospect
"I appreciate it but I'm just not there yet."
You — Graceful Disqualify
"No worries at all — I appreciate you taking the time to talk. Just out of curiosity, is there a point in the future where you see yourself wanting to revisit this, or is it just not the right fit right now?"
Graceful exit. Never burn a bridge. "Not the right fit" gives them an easy out while keeping the door open. Follow up in 30–60 days.

Tonality Guide

Jeremy Miner says tonality accounts for 50% of results. The words are 50% — how you say them is the other 50%. This guide covers every stage of the call.

REFERENCE — Not a stage, a cheat sheet
Tone by Stage
COLD OPENER (Warm-Up)
Confused · Relaxed · Slightly unsure
Low energy. Natural pauses. "Um…" and "uh…" are intentional. You are not sharp or energetic. You sound like you almost called the wrong number.
CONNECTION (Stage 1)
Warm · Genuine · Unhurried
Friendly but not salesy. Slow down 20%. Think of a trusted advisor, not a rep. "I appreciate that" — say it and mean it.
SITUATION (Stage 2)
Curious · Neutral · Like a journalist
You are genuinely learning, not fishing for angles. Head slightly tilted. "Got it… and…" — acknowledge, then move to the next question without pressure.
PROBLEM AWARENESS (Stage 3)
Empathetic · Slightly slower · Head-nodding
"I hear that a lot…" said slowly builds enormous rapport. When they share pain, pause for 2–3 seconds before responding. That silence communicates that you actually heard them.
SOLUTION AWARENESS (Stage 4)
Reflective · Open · Inviting
After "what would it look like for you if…" — go completely silent. Let them paint the picture. Do not fill the silence. This pause is where they sell themselves.
CONSEQUENCE (Stage 5)
Heavier · Slower · Let it land
Drop your voice slightly. Slow down noticeably. After "where do you see yourself in 12 months?" pause for 3–4 seconds. The weight of the question should settle before they answer.
COMMITMENT (Stage 6)
Calm · Steady · No urgency
You are not excited. You are settled. This communicates that you don't need the sale. Zero desperation. The energy of someone who has plenty of other clients and is genuinely checking fit.
PRESENTATION (Stage 7)
Confident · Conversational · Not a pitch
Slightly more energy, but never "salesperson energy." Think: a friend who works in trucking telling you about something that helped them. Check in every 2–3 sentences. Never monologue.
OBJECTIONS (Stage 8)
Never defensive · Curious · Pause before Layer 2
When they object — breathe. Pause 2 seconds. Then validate. If you respond instantly you sound rehearsed and defensive. Slow responses communicate confidence and genuine curiosity.
CLOSE (Stage 9)
Calm · Zero desperation · Unhurried
The close should feel like a natural conclusion to a good conversation. You are not closing — you are confirming what they already told you they wanted. Slow, warm, certain.

Cold Open — "Confused Old Man" Technique

Pattern interrupt: NO digas tu nombre + empresa + razón de llamada. Eso activa la guardia. En cambio, sonas confundido — como si no estuvieras seguro de hablar con la persona correcta. El prospecto baja la guardia y viene a tu rescate. Luego plantas el "possible hidden gap" con consecuencia negativa.

TONE: Confundido · Bajo · Pausado · "um…" y "uh…" intencionales — NO sharp, NO excited
❌ Don't say this
"Hi, my name is [Name], I'm with QuickGS Logistics, and the reason why I called you was…" — this triggers fight-or-flight. They've heard this 20 times today. Guard goes up instantly.
Step 1 — Confused opener (they pick up)
You
"Yeah… is this uh… is this [Owner's Name]?"
Upward inflection — like you're not sure you have the right person. This alone stops the "not interested" reflex before it starts.
Prospect
"Yeah, who's this?"
You
"Oh hey… yeah it's just uh… [Your Name]… I was wondering if you could possibly um… help me out for a moment."
The word "just" is intentional — it downplays you. On a cold call you have zero credibility already. "Just" signals it's not a big deal → they lower their guard. "Help me out" triggers human instinct to assist.
Prospect
"Uh… sure, how can I help you?"
Step 2 — "Not sure if you're the right person" + Hidden Gap + Consequence
You
"Well… and I'm not sure if you're the right person I should be talking to… but I called to see who would be responsible for… overseeing any possible hidden gaps in like your dispatching — like finding loads and negotiating rates with brokers — that could be causing you guys to keep leaving money on the table every month… uh… who should I be talking to about that?"
"Possible" = neutral word — they can't say "we don't have that" because you said possible. "Could be" = neutral, not "is." "Who should I be talking to" = if they ARE the owner, they can't say "that's not me" without looking bad. They say "that's me."
Su reacción — elige el escenario
Prospect
"That's me — what's this about?"
You
"Oh good… yeah so um… we work with owner-operators on the dispatching side — finding loads, negotiating rates, handling the paperwork… uh… I'm not even sure if what we do would make sense for your situation or if you've already got that covered… would you be opposed to me asking you just a couple questions to find out?"
"I'm not even sure if what we do would make sense" = push them away → they pull you back. "Would you be opposed" = uses their instinct to say no in your favor. → Move to Stage 1: Connection.
Prospect
"What do you mean by hidden gaps?"
